WHO YOU ARE WRITING TO — The bar manager
  Runs a 40-to-90-seat spirits-led room, already pours the serve five times a night, and buys on cost per pour and consistency.
  In full: The person who signs off what goes on the list at an owner-operated bar or a restaurant with a proper back bar — one of about 9,036 café and restaurant businesses in a market growing at 1.0% a year with venues closing in it. They are measured on gross profit percentage and on consistency, do the stocktake on a Sunday night, and already carry six suppliers they did not choose. They are not looking for a seventh. They are looking for one line on the list to stop costing them arguments.
  THE JOB THEY ARE HIRING THIS FOR: Make the dirty martini the same drink on a Tuesday as on a Saturday, at a cost per pour they can put in a spreadsheet and defend to an owner.
  THEIR WEEK — write into a moment in it, not into the abstract:
    - Monday, 10am: Ordering. Six supplier emails, one spreadsheet, and a delivery window that has to fit around a lunch service.
    - Tuesday to Thursday, 4.30pm: The pre-service line-up. Ten minutes where the team tastes what is new and the manager decides whether it is worth the rail space.
    - Friday and Saturday, service: Five to ten of the serve a night, poured by whoever is on. Consistency is a staffing problem, not a recipe problem.
    - Sunday night: Stocktake and the GP conversation with the owner. Every line on the list is defended or dropped here.
  WHAT THEY DO INSTEAD TODAY — this is what you are actually competing with:
    - The catering jar of olives, drained for its liquid — costs them: An unmeasured pour, a different jar every fortnight, and a serve whose cost nobody can name.
    - Making a house brine in-house — costs them: Prep hours, a different result each batch, and no food-safety record for the thing that goes in the glass.
    - Leaving the serve off the list — costs them: The drink is being ordered by name elsewhere. A spirits-led room without it looks like the bar two doors down.
  WHERE THEY ARE, AND WHAT THEY ARE READING THERE:
    - Behind their own bar, between 4 and 5pm — Nothing. This is why the approach is a person and two bottles, not an email campaign.
    - Their inbox, at 10am on a Monday — Order confirmations and price lists.

    - The distributor rep who already visits — A folder of things with better margins than ours.
  WHAT TRIGGERS A PURCHASE — a piece that does not sit on one of these is decoration:

    - The Sunday GP conversation named this drink. → Cost the serve properly for the first time.

  WHAT STOPS THEM — answer one of these rather than adding an adjective:

HOW TO USE THIS
  - Write to the one person in WHO YOU ARE WRITING TO. If a sentence would
    also suit the other customer, it is too wide — narrow it.
  - Name the customer's problem before naming the product.
  - One idea per sentence, and one call to action per piece.
  - Never make a claim about what brine does to a person. Positioning is
    culinary: a mixer and a cooking acid. legal/COPY-RULES.md is binding on
    every word, including yours, and is enforced by a scanner.
  - Alcohol may be named as an ingredient and an occasion. Never as a
    quantity, never as an effect, never aimed at anyone under 18.
