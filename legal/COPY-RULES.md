## 0. The rule in one paragraph

HALF SOUR is a **food** in law. Everything we say about it is regulated as a claim about food. We sell
it as a **drink, a mixer and a cooking ingredient** — a flavour and an occasion — and we say nothing at
all about what it does to a human body. We do not say it hydrates, replaces anything, helps anything,
prevents anything, or is good for anyone. We describe taste, use, craft, provenance and ritual. If a
sentence would still be true of a bottle of vermouth or a jar of olives, it is probably safe. If it
would only be worth writing because the reader might feel better afterwards, delete it.

## 1. Why the law bites — the three layers

### Layer 1 — the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code

The Code applies to food "sold, processed or handled for sale in Australia or New Zealand"
(**Standard 1.1.1—3(1)**). Its provisions have force in New Zealand because they are incorporated in or
adopted under the **Food Act 2014 (NZ)** (Note 2 to Standard 1.2.7; Standard 1.1.1—3(1) Note 1).

The Code defines **claim** extremely broadly:

> "_claim_ means an express or implied statement, representation, design or information in relation to a
> food or a property of food which is not mandatory in this Code."
> — Standard 1.1.2—2, reproduced in Note 1 to Standard 1.2.7—2

Note the words **implied** and **design**. A pack shot next to a gym bag is a claim. A landing-page
section headed "Why it works" is a claim. An emoji can be a claim.

And **advertising is caught as hard as the label**:

> "If this Code prohibits a label on or relating to food from including a statement, information, a
> design or a representation, an advertisement for that food must not include that statement,
> information, design or representation."
> — **Standard 1.2.1—23**

So there is no "we only said it on Instagram" defence.

### Layer 2 — the Fair Trading Act 1986 (NZ)

- **s 9** — "No person shall, in trade, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to
  mislead or deceive."
- **s 10** — conduct liable to mislead the public as to the **nature, manufacturing process,
  characteristics, suitability for a purpose, or quantity** of goods.
- **s 12A** — "A person must not, in trade, make an unsubstantiated representation." A representation is
  unsubstantiated if the person "does not, when the representation is made, have **reasonable grounds**
  for the representation, **irrespective of whether the representation is false or misleading**."
- **s 13(a)** — false or misleading representation that goods are of a particular **kind, standard,
  quality, grade, quantity, composition**.
- **s 13(e)** — false or misleading representation that goods have any **sponsorship, approval,
  endorsement, performance characteristics, accessories, uses, or benefits**.
- **s 13(i)** — false or misleading representation concerning "the existence, exclusion, or effect of any
  condition, warranty, guarantee, right, or remedy, including … under the Consumer Guarantees Act 1993".
- **s 13(j)** — false or misleading representation concerning **place of origin**.
- **s 40(1)** — contravening s 10, s 12A or s 13 is an **offence**: fine up to **$200,000** for an
  individual, **$600,000** for a body corporate. (s 9 is not itself an offence, but is enforceable by
  injunction and damages.)

s 12A is the one that matters most day to day. It means the burden is on _us_, at the moment we hit
publish, to already hold reasonable grounds. "Nobody complained" is not a defence. Under **s 12C** only
the Commerce Commission may bring s 12A proceedings.

### Layer 3 — the Consumer Guarantees Act 1993 (NZ)

Anything we say about the product feeds the **s 8** guarantee (fit for any particular purpose the
supplier **represents** it is fit for) and the **s 9** guarantee that goods correspond with their
description. Promise "settles your stomach" and you have manufactured a CGA failure you must remedy at
your own cost under s 18. See `terms-of-sale.md`.

## 2. What HALF SOUR is, in regulatory terms

A non-alcoholic, shelf-stable-or-chilled vegetable brine sold packaged for retail. That places it in the
Code's general food rules and, for the New Zealand operating licence, in a food sector under the Food
Act 2014 — see `food-safety-checklist.md`, which explains why the sector classification is not settled
and must be confirmed with MPI.

Two consequences run straight into copy:

1. **We are not an "electrolyte drink" and must never be described as one.** "Electrolyte drink" is a
   _**prescribed name**_ under **Standard 2.6.2—12(1)**, and **Standard 2.6.2—11(1)** says "A food that is
   sold as an electrolyte drink … must be an electrolyte drink". An electrolyte drink must contain
   "no less than 10 mmol/L of sodium" **and** 20–100 g/L of dextrose, fructose, glucose syrup,
   maltodextrin or sucrose (**2.6.2—11(2)**). Pickle brine contains none of those added sugars, so
   calling it an electrolyte drink is not merely a marketing risk — it is a **compositional breach of
   Standard 2.6.2**, on top of everything else.
2. **We are not a sports food.** Formulated supplementary sports foods are a separate regulated category
   (Standard 2.9.4) with their own composition and labelling rules. Sports positioning would drag us
   into it.

### Group A — health effects and body functions → **health claims**

A "**health claim**" is any claim that "states, suggests or implies that a food or a property of food
has, or may have, a **health effect**" (Standard 1.1.2—2, in Note 1 to Standard 1.2.7—2). A "**health
effect**" expressly includes an effect on a biochemical process, a physiological process, a functional
process, growth and development, **physical performance**, **mental performance**, or "a disease,
disorder or condition".

A health claim may **not** be made unless (a) the food **meets the NPSC** (nutrient profiling scoring
criterion, Standard 1.2.7—18(1)(a), method in Schedule 5 per 1.2.7—25) **and** (b) the claim is either a
pre-approved food–health relationship in the tables to **S4—4/S4—5**, or a relationship the maker has
established by systematic review under Schedule 6 and **notified to the FSANZ CEO**
(Standard 1.2.7—18(3)(b), 1.2.7—19). HALF SOUR has done none of this. Therefore **every** health claim is
unlawful for us today.

> #### Carve-out: "cure" in the food sense is approved brand vocabulary
>
> A half-sour **is** a cucumber **cured** in salt brine rather than vinegar. That is the name
> rationale and it is the correct technical term. **"cured in brine", "a three-week cure",
> "cure time", "cure times", "curing salt" and "cured with dill" are all approved and must stay
> approved.** They are a different word from the medical "cure" that happens to share a spelling.
>

> `cures <named ailment>`, `cure-all`, `curative`. **Do not re-broaden it.** A rule that fires on
> correct copy is as damaging as a missing rule, because people learn to ignore the scanner — and a
> scanner nobody reads catches nothing. This carve-out is tested in
> `legal/scripts/test-audit.mjs` and any change to TH-02 must keep those tests green.

### Group D — nutrition content claims: banned **unless the Code's condition is actually met and evidenced**

A **nutrition content claim** is a claim about the presence or absence of energy, protein, carbohydrate,
fat and their components, dietary fibre, salt, sodium, potassium, minerals, vitamins, a biologically
active substance, or about glycaemic index/load (**Standard 1.1.2—9(1)**, reproduced at Note 2 to
Standard 1.2.7—2).

Where a property of food appears in **Column 1 of the table to S4—3**, a claim about it may only be made
if the food meets the **general claim conditions in Column 2**, and if a **Column 3 descriptor** (or a
synonym of one) is used, the food must also meet the **Column 4 condition** (Standard 1.2.7—12(2)–(3)).
Where the property is _not_ in S4—3, the claim may only state that the food contains / does not contain
it, or state a specified amount — and may not use any descriptor other than one indicating absence
(Standard 1.2.7—13).

**These are banned by default.** They may only be used after the founder holds a current laboratory
analysis of the actual production recipe showing the threshold is met, and that analysis is filed and
cited in the copy brief. Approve a specific line with the marker described in §8.

### Group F — endorsements, testimonials and third-party voices

(while on sale, plus 2 years). An endorsement must still comply with **1.2.7—8** and must not refer to a
serious disease (1.2.7—24(3)).

| END-02 | Publishing a customer review, quote, UGC repost, influencer caption or comment reply that contains any Group A–D term | Republication is publication. Std 1.2.1—23; FTA s 9. Moderate before reposting; do not "like" or pin such comments. |
| END-03 | `#pickleback` etc. is fine, but `#pickleforcramps`-style tags                                                         | Same as above.                                                                                                      |

### Group G — place of origin and provenance (PROV-01 … PROV-05)

HALF SOUR has decided to build supplier provenance into its marketing: named growers, named
regions. That is a good story and a real legal exposure, because a provenance line is a
**representation about goods** and every one of them must be **literally true before it ships**.

The governing provisions:

- **Fair Trading Act 1986 s 13(j)** — no person shall, in trade, "make a false or misleading
  representation concerning the **place of origin** of goods or services."
- **s 12A** — even a _true-sounding_ provenance line breaches the Act if we do not hold reasonable
  grounds for it at the moment we publish. A supply relationship that is planned, discussed or
  hoped-for is **not** reasonable grounds.
- **s 13(a)** — a representation about the goods' "kind, standard, quality, grade, quantity,
  composition" also catches "made from Tairāwhiti kawakawa" when the kawakawa is from somewhere else.
- **s 40(1)** — each of those is an offence carrying a fine up to $200,000 for an individual.

**"Made in Aotearoa New Zealand" has a specific legal meaning.** It is a statement about **where the
goods were substantially transformed** — where the brine was actually made and bottled — not about
where the brand lives, where the founder lives, or where the idea came from. If the cucumbers are
imported and the brine is blended and bottled in Auckland, "Made in New Zealand" is defensible and
"New Zealand grown" is not. They are different claims and must not be swapped for each other.

| ID                     | Pattern                                                                                                                        | Rule                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| PROV-01 _(restricted)_ | `made in New Zealand`, `made in Aotearoa`, `NZ made`, `bottled in…`, `product of…`                                             | A claim about **where transformation occurred**. Approve only with a one-line note of what is actually made where. For Australian sale this additionally engages the ACL country-of-origin information standard — see §7. |
| PROV-02 _(restricted)_ | `grown in/at/by`, `harvested in`, `wild-harvested`, `foraged`, `picked`, `sourced from`, `single-origin`, `estate-grown`       | A claim about **where the ingredient came from**. Approve only with the supplier's name and the date the relationship was confirmed.                                                                                      |
| PROV-03 _(restricted)_ | `our grower`, `our growers`, `our farmers`, `our suppliers`, `our whānau`, `family farm`, `we work with…`                      | A claim that a **named relationship exists**. If the relationship does not exist yet, publishing it is a false representation even if the deal is close.                                                                  |
| PROV-04 _(restricted)_ | Named New Zealand places: `Tairāwhiti`, `Gisborne`, `Te Tai Tokerau`, `Kaitaia`, `Marlborough`, `Hawke's Bay`, `Canterbury`, … | A specific place name in product copy **reads as a provenance claim** whether or not it is phrased as one. Approve it with a supplier and a date, or take the place name out.                                             |
| PROV-05 **banned**     | `TODO`, `TBC`, `TBD`, `PLACEHOLDER`, `FIXME` on the same line as any sourcing word                                             | Aspirational sourcing copy must never reach a published surface. Finish it or delete it — do not ship it with a note beside it.                                                                                           |

**Standing instruction for aspirational sourcing.** Write it in a planning document, not in product
copy. Product copy carries only what is contracted and shipping today. If a supplier line is going to
be true in three months, publish it in three months — the brand loses nothing and gains a launch beat.

> ⚠️ **Live issue flagged 2026-08-27:** one product description names a **Tairāwhiti kawakawa
> grower who is not yet a supplier**. Under FTA ss 12A and 13(j) that line is a false representation
> the moment the page is public, and half-sour.vercel.app is public today. It must be removed or
> made true before the next deploy. This is not a stylistic note.

**Can the script catch this on its own? Partly — and it is important to be honest about which part.**

- **What the script _can_ do reliably:** detect that a _provenance-shaped sentence exists_ and force
  a human to sign it off. That is what PROV-01 to PROV-04 do — they are `restricted`, so the build
  fails until someone writes `copy-audit-approved: PROV-02 (Supplier name, confirmed 2026-08-11)`.
  The marker is the audit trail. It also catches the specific, common failure of **draft provenance
  shipping with a TODO next to it** (PROV-05, banned outright).
- **What the script _cannot_ do:** know whether the grower is real. No regex can verify a supply
  relationship. Truth is a fact about the world, not a property of the string.
- **Therefore the split is deliberate:** the script guarantees that **no provenance claim reaches
  production unreviewed**; the **human checklist in §4A** guarantees that the review means something.
  Anyone who writes an approval marker without doing the check has converted an accidental breach
  into a knowing one under FTA s 12A — which is worse, and is the only thing that makes this design
  fail.

### Group H — No 04 WILD, kawakawa and native botanicals

**No 04 WILD (kawakawa, kelp, lemon) is the highest-risk product in the range** and needs saying out
loud so that nobody writes wellness copy for it later.

Kawakawa carries strong **rongoā Māori** associations. Every one of those associations is a
therapeutic claim, and **Standard 1.2.7—8** bans therapeutic claims absolutely — there is no
NPSC gateway, no notification route, no "we were only quoting tradition" exception. Kelp carries the
same problem via iodine and "mineral" framing (see NCC-18 and EL-06).

| ID                     | Rule                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| WILD-01 **banned**     | `rongoā`, `rongoa`, `traditional Māori medicine`, `traditional medicine`, `medicinal plant/herb/leaf`, `healing plant/herb/leaf`, `bush medicine`. Prohibited as a therapeutic claim under Std 1.2.7—8(a)–(b), and separately because it presses mātauranga Māori into service as a sales device. Do not write it, do not repost it, do not agree with a customer who writes it. |
| WILD-02 _(restricted)_ | `kawakawa`, `kelp`, `seaweed`, `karengo`, `horopito`, `harakeke`, `mānuka`, `pikopiko`. Permitted **only** as an ingredient name or a flavour note — "kawakawa, kelp and lemon", "peppery, faintly numbing, maritime". Never with a health, wellness or rongoā frame.                                                                                                            |

Two further obligations attach to WILD specifically, and they are food-safety obligations, not copy
ones — see `food-safety-checklist.md`:

1. **Novel food status.** Standard 1.5.1 defines a _novel food_ as a _non-traditional food_ requiring
   safety assessment, and a _non-traditional food_ includes "a food that does not have a history of
   human consumption in Australia or New Zealand". Kawakawa and kelp in a bottled beverage need this
   question answered — by FSANZ or MPI, in writing — **before** WILD is sold. It is not a question a
   copywriter can settle.
2. **Tikanga and sourcing.** If kawakawa is wild-harvested, provenance (PROV-02/03/04) and the
   permission of the landowner or hapū are both real. Get it in writing before it appears in copy.

## 4A. The provenance checklist — the part no script can do

Before writing `copy-audit-approved: PROV-0x` on any line, the founder (not a writer, not an agent)
answers all five. Keep the answers in one file — a spreadsheet is fine — with a date against each.

1. **Does the supplier relationship exist today?** Not "we spoke", not "they're keen". Is there an
   order, an invoice, or a written agreement? If no: the line does not ship.
2. **Is the ingredient in the batch that is actually on sale?** Provenance attaches to the bottle a
   customer receives, not to the recipe in principle. A batch made from a different lot needs
   different copy or no copy.
3. **Is the place name correct at the right level?** "Grown in Tairāwhiti" and "grown in Gisborne"
   are different claims. So are "New Zealand grown" (about the ingredient) and "Made in New Zealand"
   (about where transformation happened).
4. **Do we have permission to name them?** Naming a grower, a farm, a whānau or a hapū in marketing
   needs their agreement in writing — a legal point and a relationship point. For wild-harvested
   native plants, that includes the landowner or hapū whose land it came from.
5. **What happens when it stops being true?** Name the person who removes the line when the supplier
   changes, and put a review date on it. Provenance copy goes stale silently; that is how honest
   businesses end up in breach of FTA s 12A.

**Division of labour, stated plainly:** the script proves that _someone looked_. This checklist is
what looking means. Neither is sufficient alone, and the marker is worthless if the checklist was
skipped — an unchecked approval marker turns an accidental misrepresentation into a knowing one.

- "Made for the drink, not the diet."

## 6. Structural rules that are not about single words

1. **Layout can be a claim.** Standard 1.2.7—2's definition of _claim_ includes a **design**. A photo of
   a runner, a heart icon, a leaf motif, a "benefits" grid, a green colour-block that reads as
   health-food packaging — all can carry an implied health claim. Ask what a reasonable consumer takes
   from the whole page, not from the sentence.
2. **Never imply by adjacency.** Do not place the NIP next to lifestyle copy about energy. Do not put
   the sodium figure in a callout box. The NIP is mandatory information and is not a claim
   (Std 1.1.2—9(2)) — pulling a number out of it _into_ marketing turns it into one (Std 1.1.2—9(3) makes
   voluntary NIP information a nutrition content claim).
3. **Questions are claims.** "Ever wondered why athletes drink pickle juice?" is a claim. So is a link to
   someone else's article that makes the claim.
4. **Comparisons.** Any comparative nutrition claim must state the reference food and the size of the
   difference _together with the claim_ (Std 1.2.7—16(1)) — and Std 1.2.7—9 bans vitamin/mineral
   comparisons outright.
5. **The form of the food.** A nutrition content claim must be stated together with a statement of the
   form of the food to which it relates, unless that is the food as sold (Std 1.2.7—11). Relevant if we
   ever describe a diluted serve.
6. **Everything on the label is also governed by** `food-safety-checklist.md`. Copy cannot override a
   mandatory label element or crowd it out of legibility (Std 1.2.1—24: legible, prominent, in English,
   contrasting distinctly with the background).
7. **Do not answer the question we refuse to answer.** If a customer asks "will this help my cramps?",
   the answer is a redirection, never a hedged yes. See §9.

## 7. Australia — the deltas when we expand

- **The Food Standards Code is the same instrument in both countries** (Standard 1.1.1—3(1)). Everything
  in §§3–6 applies unchanged in Australia.
- **Australian Consumer Law** (Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), Schedule 2) replaces the Fair
  Trading Act as the general prohibition: **s 18** (misleading or deceptive conduct), **s 29** (false or
  misleading representations about goods, including as to standard, quality, composition, performance
  characteristics, uses or benefits, and place of origin), **s 33** (misleading conduct as to the nature,
  manufacturing process, characteristics or suitability for purpose of goods). The **consumer
  guarantees** in ACL ss 51–59 are the analogue of the CGA and are likewise non-excludable (ACL s 64).
  ⚠️ _Section numbers in this bullet are from general knowledge of the ACL and have **not** been verified
  against a fetched copy of Schedule 2 in this pass — confirm before publishing Australian-facing copy._
- **Therapeutic claims are policed harder.** Making a therapeutic representation can bring a product
  within the **Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 (Cth)** and the TGA's advertising code, in addition to
  Standard 1.2.7—8. ⚠️ _Not verified against a fetched source in this pass._
- **Country of origin labelling is mandatory in Australia and is not a marketing choice.** It is imposed
  by an information standard made under the ACL, not by the Food Standards Code — Standard 1.2.11 was
  repealed for that reason (see the amendment history to Standard 1.2.1, F2016L01367, which repealed the
  country-of-origin references in 1.2.1—7 and 1.2.1—14 with effect from 1 July 2018). Whether pickle
  brine is a "priority" or "non-priority" food determines whether a full bar-chart label is required.
  ⚠️ **This must be confirmed with the ACCC before any Australian sale** — see `README.md`.
- **Privacy** is a separate regime: the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and the Australian Privacy Principles.
  Small businesses under a turnover threshold have historically been exempt, but that exemption has been
  under active reform. ⚠️ **Get advice before the first Australian order** — do not assume the exemption.

## 8. Enforcement — the audit script, the markers and the exemption registry

```
node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs <path> [<path>...]
node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs --list              # print every rule, its regex and its surface scope
node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs --surfaces          # print the surface model (§8A)
node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs --json app/         # machine-readable output for CI
node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs --no-exemptions .   # ignore the registry, see everything
node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs --show-suppressed . # list what was NOT raised, and why
node legal/scripts/test-audit.mjs                     # self-test
```

Exit codes: **0** = clean, **1** = at least one finding, **2** = usage error.
Output is `file:line:column  SEVERITY  [RULE-ID]  "match"` followed by the surface the text sits on,
why the rule reaches that surface, the reason and the source.

**Two severities.** `banned` never passes. `restricted` fails the build until a human records that
the condition was checked. Both fail; only one is clearable.

**Escape hatches — use sparingly and honestly:**

| Marker                                                                            | Effect                                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `copy-audit-approved: <RULE-ID>` on the line, or the nearest non-blank line above | Clears **restricted** findings for that rule on that line. **No effect on banned rules.**        |
| `copy-audit-ignore` on the line, or the line above                                | Skips that line entirely.                                                                        |
| `copy-audit-ignore-start` … `copy-audit-ignore-end`                               | Skips everything between them. **This is the right tool for a document's own prohibition list.** |
| `copy-audit-ignore-file` in the first 20 lines                                    | Skips the whole file.                                                                            |

**The exemption registry — `legal/copy-audit-exempt.json`.** Some files exist in order to enumerate
the forbidden words: this file, `legal/README.md`, the deliberately-dirty test fixture, and the brand
guide's "What HALF SOUR never says" / "What it is never" sections. Scanning a prohibition list with
the marketing ruleset produces noise, and **noise is the real failure mode** — people stop reading
the output, and then the scanner catches nothing. So those files are registered, each with a written
reason and a review date. Entries can be scoped to a whole file, to named markdown **headings**, or
to named literal lines, so that the rest of the document is still scanned in full. `--no-exemptions`
shows everything. Prefer inline `copy-audit-ignore-start` / `-end` markers where the file's owner can
add them, and delete the registry entry once they have.

**The scanner reads text, not code.** `.ts/.tsx/.js/.jsx/.mjs/.cjs` are **parsed**, and only string
literals, template-literal text and JSX text are scanned. Identifiers, property keys, import paths
and comments are not scanned at all, because they are not language addressed to a reader: a shader
uniform named `absorption`, a SHA `digest`, a reduce accumulator named `best`. Renaming those to
satisfy the scanner would make the code worse for no compliance gain, and every false finding teaches
a reader to skim the output — which is how a real one gets missed.

The remaining formats follow the same principle:

| Format                               | Scanned                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `.ts .tsx .js .jsx .mjs .cjs`        | string literals, template text, JSX text. Parsed with TypeScript.                  |
| `.json .jsonc`                       | string **values**. Object keys are identifiers (`"raw-body"`, `"best-practices"`). |
| `.md .mdx .markdown`                 | prose. A fenced code block is a code sample, not copy.                             |
| `.html .svg .xml .txt .csv .yml` &c. | the whole file.                                                                    |
| `.svelte .vue .astro`                | lexical fallback — comments masked, identifier-shaped matches skipped.             |

Two consequences worth knowing, because they are boundaries rather than accidents:

- **Copy inside a fenced code block is not scanned.** If you need an example line of customer-facing
  copy audited, quote it as prose or as a blockquote, not inside triple backticks.
- **A string used as an object key is not scanned.** `{ "gut health": … }` is read as an identifier.
  Copy does not live in keys; if it ever does, the fix is to move it, not to loosen the parser.

The parse needs `typescript`, which is already a devDependency. If it is missing the scanner says so
on stderr and falls back to the lexical mask — it never silently changes what it reads.

**A `copy-audit-approved` marker is a record that a human checked the condition.** Writing one
without checking is worse than the original problem, because it converts an accident into a knowing
misrepresentation under FTA s 12A. The same is true of adding a registry entry to silence a finding
you have not read.

## 8A. Surfaces — where a sentence is published, and which rules therefore reach it

This section is the authority for the surface model. `legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs` implements it and
`node legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs --surfaces` prints it; if the two ever disagree, this file wins.

### Why there is a distinction at all

Almost every rule in §3 and §4 exists because of a statute that regulates **a representation made in
trade to a consumer**. Fair Trading Act s 9 catches misleading conduct _in trade_; ss 12A, 13(a) and
13(e) catch false or unsubstantiated representations about goods; the ASA codes govern
_advertisements_. A password-gated production dashboard that only the founder ever opens is none of
those things. "Best customers" as a panel heading above a table sorted by lifetime spend is a factual
sort order, not a claim that a product is superior to a competitor's, and there is no consumer in the
room to mislead.

Applying the advertising rules there is not merely noisy. It is actively harmful in two ways. It
pushes an engineer to rename a reduce accumulator or a table column to appease a linter, which makes
the code worse and the compliance no better. And **noise is the real failure mode**: a scanner that
cries wolf on `const best = …` is a scanner people stop reading, and then the health claim goes out.

### The two surfaces

| Surface      | What it is                                                                                                         | Rules that reach it                          |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| **consumer** | Anything a customer can read: the storefront, product pages, the copy library, brand voice, anything in `public/`. | **All of them.** This is the strict surface. |
| **internal** | Password-gated admin, libraries, adapters, tests, background jobs, schema docs, this scanner.                      | Only the rules marked _every-surface_ below. |

The classification lives as **data** in `SURFACE_MAP` in the script — a list of path prefixes, longest
match wins — so a misclassification is corrected by editing one line of that list, never by editing
the scanner's logic. **Anything unlisted is treated as consumer.** A new directory is policed
strictly until somebody decides, in writing, that it is not advertising.

### Which rules still apply internally, and why

A rule applies on **every surface** when the matched phrase is a claim about **what the product is or
does** — health, therapeutic, hydration and sport, nutrition content, certification, absolute
composition. Such a sentence is a regulated claim the moment anything lifts it out of the admin and
into a caption, and §9 of this file exists precisely because this company runs agents that draft
copy from what they read on internal screens. "Probiotics for gut health" typed into an admin
textarea is one copy-paste away from being published, and it is unlawful on arrival. That risk is
real, so those rules stay on everywhere:

> **Every surface** — Group A (`HC-01`…`HC-16`), Group B (`TH-01`…`TH-08`), Group C claims
> (`EL-01`…`EL-05`), Group D in full (`NCC-01`…`NCC-19`), `FTA-01` clean label, `FTA-02` pure /
> 100% natural, `FTA-03` chemical free, `FTA-08` environmental, `FTA-09` organic, `FTA-10` kosher,
> `END-04` Health Star Rating, `WILD-01` rongoā, `R-02` vegan / allergen-free. **57 of 82 rules.**

A rule is **consumer-only** when it judges _how a product, an offer, a price, a right, a provenance
story or a process is presented to a customer_. The rule is not about a word; it is about a
representation, and a private tool makes no representation to anybody:

> **Consumer surfaces only** — `FTA-04` superiority, `FTA-05` guarantees, `FTA-06` free shipping,
> `FTA-07` reference pricing, `FTA-11` traditional / authentic, `FTA-12` handmade / artisan,
> `FTA-14` endorsement and acceptance, `PROV-01`…`PROV-05` provenance, `WILD-02` native botanical
> naming, `EL-06` sodium as a selling point, and the §4 context checks `R-03`, `R-04`, `R-07`,
> `R-10`, `R-11`, `R-13`, `R-15`, `R-16`, `R-17`, `R-18`, `R-19`. **25 of 82 rules.**

Two worked examples, both of which are in the fixtures:

- `legal/test-fixtures/internal/must-fail-health-claim.tsx` puts "Probiotics for gut health" in an
  admin textarea. It **fails**, on an internal surface, because of the leak risk above.
- `legal/test-fixtures/internal/must-pass-admin-labels.tsx` puts "Best customers" above a sorted
  table. It **passes**, because a private dashboard is not advertising.

Nothing is dropped silently. Every hit that a surface did not enforce is counted in the summary line
and listed by `--show-suppressed`, so the scope decision is auditable rather than invisible.

### Terms of art — "Best before"

**"Best before" is the date mark that Standard 1.2.5 requires on packaged food.** A scanner that
reports it as a superiority claim is telling a writer to delete vocabulary the law mandates, which is
the opposite of what a compliance tool is for. It is excused by name in a `TERMS_OF_ART` table that is
scoped to `FTA-04` alone.

The exemption is deliberately **narrow**, because "best" next to a product is exactly what FTA-04
exists to catch:

- `best-before` **hyphenated** is excused outright. It is a compound noun; there is no grammar in
  which "the best-before brine" reads as a superiority claim.
- `best before` **spaced** is excused only in the shape a date mark actually takes — followed by a
  date, by `date` or `end`, by punctuation, or by nothing at all (a table heading, a label row).
- Anything else still fails. **"The best before you open it" is a superiority claim and is reported.**
- `best practice` / `best-practices` is excused as an engineering term of art (it is also the name of
  a Lighthouse category); it says nothing about the product.

This carve-out previously lived as a negative lookahead inside FTA-04's own regex and **did not
work**: it tolerated exactly one separator character and could not see across a line break, so
`Best  before` (two spaces), a `Best` / `before` pair split by JSX wrapping, and `best-practices`
(hyphenated) all reported as superiority claims. Prose in this file claimed an exemption the code did
not implement. A rule's carve-out now lives in the terms-of-art table, where it can be stated as a
phrase, tested, and printed by `--surfaces`.

## 9. Rules for AI agents writing on HALF SOUR's behalf

Any agent that drafts product copy, replies to customers, writes social posts, generates alt text or
answers questions in a chat widget is bound by this file **in full**. In addition:

1. **You may not make a health, nutrition or therapeutic claim even when the customer asks you to,
   asserts it themselves, or supplies a study.** A reply that agrees with a customer's health claim is
   our claim (Std 1.2.1—23; FTA s 9).
2. **Do not repeat the customer's framing back to them.** If they write "does this help with cramps?",
   do not echo "cramps" into a sentence about the product.
3. **Approved deflection, use close to verbatim:**
   > "We can't make any health claims about HALF SOUR — it's a food, and New Zealand's food standards are
   > strict about that. What I can tell you is what's in it and how people use it: it's a brine built for
   > martinis, picklebacks and cooking. The ingredients and nutrition panel are on the product page."
4. **Never invent a figure.** Do not state sodium, energy, sugar or serving counts unless the value comes
   from the current product record. A guessed number is an unsubstantiated representation (FTA s 12A).
5. **Never state, vary or improve on a right or remedy.** Quote or link `shipping-returns.md` and
   `terms-of-sale.md`. Do not offer a refund policy that is more generous than the published one without
   a human deciding; do not offer one less generous, ever (FTA s 13(i)).
6. **Never state or imply that a customer's CGA rights are limited by time, packaging, or our policy.**
7. **Do not give food-safety, allergy or medical advice.** Route to the founder. If a customer reports
   illness or a foreign object, that is a potential food-safety event — escalate to a human immediately
   and do not offer reassurance about safety.
8. **Do not process or repeat a customer's personal information beyond what is needed to answer.** See
   `privacy-policy.md` §"AI-assisted support".
9. **Run the audit on your own output** before it is published to a public surface.
10. **When in doubt, produce less.** An unsent sentence has never breached the Fair Trading Act.

## 10. Change control and open questions

- Any addition to the banned or restricted lists must be made in **both** this file and
  `legal/scripts/audit-copy.mjs`. The script is the enforcement; this file is the reasoning.
- Every new rule must declare a surface scope (`EVERY_SURFACE` or `CONSUMER_ONLY`) and be listed
  in §8A with the reasoning. Default to `EVERY_SURFACE` and argue your way down, never the reverse.
- Any change to a regex must keep `node legal/scripts/test-audit.mjs` green. The culinary
  "cure" carve-out in §3A Group B is covered by those tests specifically because it is the
  rule most likely to be re-broadened by someone who has not read this file. The same is now true
  of the `best before` date mark in §8A: the fixture that guards it is the one proving a genuine
  superiority claim **still fails**, because that is the way this carve-out would be broken.
- A false positive is fixed by narrowing the SCOPE — the surface map, the parse, a named term of
  art — never by deleting a term from a rule's list. The terms are the law; the scope is ours.
- Any new entry in `legal/copy-audit-exempt.json` needs a written reason and a review date.
- Any _removal_ from the banned list requires the evidence that makes it lawful to be filed first.
